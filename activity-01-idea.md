The problem:
Indian households buy daily doorstep services whose quantities change constantly without digital bills—milk (1L normally, 1.5L when guests visit), 20L water cans (delivered vs. empty jars returned), tiffin skips, presswala (ironing) counts, and domestic help leaves or cash advances. Families rely on wall calendars, memory, or scattered chats. At month-end, vendors and households disagree on counts ("Bhaiya, 18th ko ek hi can liya tha"), triggering awkward disputes or silent overpayments of ₹400–₹1,500 monthly.

Who has it, where and how often:
Urban and semi-urban Indian families, homemakers, working couples, shared bachelor flats/PGs, and senior citizens living independently. It occurs right at the front door or kitchen counter 2 to 5 times every single day during 5-second handoffs, and hurts most painfully between the 1st and 5th of every month when 4–6 vendor bills arrive together.

The voice-first product:
RozHisaab is a voice-first daily doorstep ledger for Indian homes.
Step 1 — Speak in 3 seconds: Closing the door, the user taps one button and speaks naturally in Hindi, Hinglish, or their regional language: "Aaj doodh dedh litre liya, 2 water can aaye aur 1 khali diya, aur didi chhutti pe hain" (1.5L milk, 2 cans in, 1 empty returned, maid on leave).
Step 2 — Multi-vendor auto-logging: RozHisaab splits that single sentence into structured entries per vendor (Milk +1.5L, Water +2/-1 jar balance, Maid: absent), updates running monthly totals, and replies with a 2-second spoken confirmation.
Step 3 — Voice Q&A and clean settlement: Anytime, users ask, "Is mahine presswale ke kitne kapde bache hain aur paani ka bill kitna hua?" (pending ironed clothes and this month's water bill). RozHisaab speaks back the exact date-wise summary and generates a bilingual WhatsApp-ready bill card for instant UPI settlement.

Why voice comes first:
Speaking is not an add-on—it is the only interface that survives daily Indian doorstep reality. First, handoffs happen while holding milk packets, laundry, or keys before rushing out; unlocking a phone to tap menus and type 3–5 tiny entries fails within days because manual effort outweighs a ₹50 transaction. Second, one messy 4-second spoken sentence logs multiple vendors, empty-jar returns, and absences simultaneously—what forms take 45 seconds of tapping to enter. Third, elders and homemakers speak code-mixed Hinglish or regional terms ("dedh litre", "kal tiffin band") far more naturally than typing English UI fields. Hands-free voice input plus spoken answers make RozHisaab a habit that actually sticks.
